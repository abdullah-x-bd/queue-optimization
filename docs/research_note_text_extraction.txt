                                                Abstract

         We extend a deterministic, finite-population queue-clearance model in five directions. For
     the original row family, we determine exactly how many counters must be used by an optimal
     design, prove sharp portal counts in several parameter regimes, and obtain a one-portal gap
     elsewhere when the source admits at least two people per tick. A separate finite candidate-graph
     formulation chooses waiting cells, corridors, counters, and optional discrete counter placements,
     with heterogeneous processing durations and construction and area constraints. We compute
     exact finite-instance frontiers and examine receiving rules, corridor capacity, decentralized
     control, and random durations. The algebraic clearance formula is situated within established
     max-plus and production-system theory. The proposed contribution is the sparse finite-horizon
     attainment result under the stated physical constraints, not a new general theory of queues or a
     universal optimal floorplan.

Status of claims. The structural theorems below have explicit mathematical proofs and con-
structive schedules. The finite-instance optima are certified by integer optimization with zero
reported gap, with separate integer replay of every feasible trace. Infeasibility certificates rely on
the numerical MILP solver; they are not independently checked rational proof objects. The novelty
claim is deliberately narrower than a claim of priority: the reviewed literature does not establish
that these exact sparse constructions have appeared before, but this review is not evidence that
they have never appeared.
Main deliverables. General portal bounds; a heterogeneous finite-graph solver; a complete
illustrative budget frontier; spatial topology and discrete placement examples; exact enumeration of
a two-point service-time distribution; and source code, data, schedule witnesses, and reproduction
instructions.

where every person must complete exactly one service and then reach the absorbing exit. The
layout G and the admission, routing, service-start, and release policy π are jointly chosen. Here
B and A denote construction and area budgets. Cells have finite storage, interfaces have finite
movement capacities, and downstream saturation can prevent a completed job from leaving its

Population and service times alone do not specify G. A corridor-shaped footprint and an open hall
can admit different optimal designs with the same N and pj . Our exact global statements therefore
always name the admissible design space. The row-family results optimize all subsets of specified
interfaces. The generic solver optimizes all subgraphs of a supplied spatial candidate graph, and
can also select among supplied counter-placement alternatives. A continuous, unrestricted floorplan
optimization is a different problem.


1.1    The original row family

There are m rows. Each contains two approach cells Aj , Bj , a one-person counter Cj , and a
one-person departure cell Ej . A finite source X0 contains all N people initially. Departure cells
lead to a common merge M , then to the absorbing exit X1 .
The optional physical portals are X0 → Aj , Aj → Bj , and the adjacent lateral interfaces in the A and
B columns. Each lateral interface permits either direction but has a combined movement capacity
of one per tick. There are 4m − 2 candidate portals. Service interfaces and links Ej → M → X1
are fixed and are excluded from this portal count.
Approach cells have storage two. Each has sending and receiving capacity one. The source can
admit up to Q0 people per tick, with Q0 ≥ 1. Its storage K0 must satisfy K0 ≥ N because everyone
is initially present there. If K0 < N , this initial condition is infeasible; an external arrival or staging
process would be an additional model input. Source storage and source admission capacity are
distinct parameters.
Movement from one ordinary cell to another takes one tick. Service begins when a person is removed
from Bj at integer time t. Service lasts p full ticks. A completed person can be released at time
t + p or later, reaches Ej one tick after release, and can eventually move through M to X1 . A
release and a new service start at the same counter may occur at the same time. This gives earliest
possible exit time
                                            D = p + 5.

The strict synchronous convention measures receiving space before any movement in that tick:

                                        Ov (t) ≤ min{nv (t), Qv },                                       (1)
                                        Iv (t) ≤ min{Kv − nv (t), Qv },                                  (2)
                                   nv (t + 1) = nv (t) + Iv (t) − Ov (t).                                (3)

A full cell cannot receive during the tick in which it sends. All results in Sections 2 and 3 use this
convention, unit corridor rates, and the storage sizes just stated. The integer ticks are model units,
not empirically calibrated seconds.

2.1      The max-plus relation, and what it does not prove by itself

The optimal exit sequence is represented by

          Ci = D + (i − 1)δ      (1 ≤ i ≤ m),       Cn = max{Cn−1 + δ, Cn−m + b}              (n > m).

Solving this recurrence gives (4). Its long-run cycle time is max(δ, b/m), the usual maximum-cycle-
mean form. This recurrence represents the optimum; it is not asserted to govern every feasible
delayed or backlogged schedule. The algebra alone does not show that a sparse, spatially feasible
layout attains it. The constructions and connectivity lower bounds below supply that additional
result.

optimum meets the exit lower bound, then

                                                   2r∗ ,      Q0 = 1,

Proof. All exits must occur at D, D + 1, . . . , D + N − 1. The first r∗ require distinct counters
because b ≥ r∗ . The first two service starts must be no later than times 2 and 3. A single entry A
cell cannot supply both: with its unit receiving and sending rates, its earliest second job reaches
a different counter’s B cell only at time 4. Therefore at least two A cells are needed, giving the
connectivity bound L ≥ r∗ + 2.
If Q0 = 1, the first person must leave the source at time 0 and take a direct two-move approach.
Inductively the ith of the first r∗ exiting people must leave at time i − 1 and also take a direct
approach: earlier departure slots are already occupied and any extra approach move misses its
deadline. These distinct counters therefore require r∗ different direct entry/forward pairs. The
direct construction matches 2r∗ .

There is a useful further bound outside the exit-limited regime. Suppose b > m, N > m, and
KM ≥ 2, so r∗ = m. Write

Proposition 6 (A finite-population prefix constraint). If Q0 = 1, then L∗ ≥ m + h. Consequently,
if m divides N , L∗ = 2m. If Q0 ≥ 2 and h ≥ 2, then L∗ = m + 2.

Proof. The deadline is D + qb + h − 1. Since h − 1 < b, no counter can produce more than q + 1
exiting jobs. At least h counters must produce q + 1. Their final outputs cannot precede D + qb;
exactly h output slots remain. Thus these h outputs occur consecutively, forcing their corresponding
first service starts no later than 2, 3, . . . , h + 1. The source-prefix argument above gives at least h
distinct A cells when Q0 = 1, hence m + h portals. With Q0 ≥ 2 and h ≥ 2, the first two forced
starts require two A cells; the two-chain construction attains m + 2.

Proposition 7 (A service-limited regime with exact one-feeder sparsity). If m ≥ 2, KM ≥ 2,
Q0 ≥ 1, b ≥ 2m − 1, and N = qm + 1 with q ≥ 1, then L∗ = m + 1.

Proof. Use the one-feeder chain. In round k, dispatch counter h + 1’s job at kb + h and start it at
2 + kb + 2h. Take q complete rounds and one final job at the root. The last job from one full round
exits at offset 2m − 2, while the next root’s exit is at offset b. The assumption b ≥ 2m − 1 gives the
required one-tick merge separation. All other finite-cell checks are those of the chain construction.
The final root exits at D + qb, exactly the optimum; connectivity supplies the matching lower
bound.

Population size matters. For m = 3, p = 4, KM = 2, N = 4 has L∗ = 4: service starts at times
2 at the central counter, 4 and 5 at its neighbors, and 6 at the central counter. Exits are 9, 11, 12,
13, reaching T ∗ = 13. The same m, p, KM with N = 12 has L∗ = 5 when Q0 ≥ 2. A formula for
portal count must therefore retain N as an input.

4.3    The general model and its exactness

The supplied program accepts any finite candidate graph whose ordinary cells are partitioned into
pre-service and post-service cells. Counters connect the two parts; the source belongs to the first
and the exit to the second. The solver chooses cell-build variables hv , physical-portal variables ye ,
and counter-build or placement variables wj . Ordinary flow cannot cross from the first part to the
second. Thus every exiting person must undergo service exactly once.
For a deadline H, the principal integer variables are occupancies nv (t), movement counts fe (t),
service starts sj (t), releases rj (t), and binary counter occupancies oj (t). In addition to cell balances
and sending/receiving constraints, the model imposes

An empty sum is zero. The release inequality and one-person occupancy enforce nonpreemptive

receiving space. Build variables also activate their required endpoints.
For alternative locations of a physical counter, the sum of its placement variables is at most one.
The sum of counter variables at a candidate site is also at most one. These constraints allow static
counter placement to be optimized without duplicating a physical counter. Longer corridors can be
represented by additional cells; non-unit rates are direct model inputs.
Proposition 8 (Finite-model completeness). For a fixed integer horizon, the formulation is feasible
if and only if the stated discrete physical model admits a layout and schedule meeting that horizon
and the resource bounds. Therefore minimizing the feasible horizon finds a global optimum over the
supplied candidate cells, portals, and placement alternatives.

Proof. A physical schedule supplies integer occupancies, flows, starts, releases, and build decisions
satisfying every constraint. Conversely, replay integer flows using only people present at the
beginning of each tick. Sending bounds prevent using fresh arrivals in the same tick; receiving
bounds preserve storage. At a counter, the cumulative eligibility inequality and single occupancy
match each release to a sufficiently old service start. Inductively all moves and service events
are executable. Conservation and the phase partition imply that all N people reaching the exit
have each been served exactly once. Counter and site groups enforce the specified placement
restrictions.

This is an established time-indexed integer-optimization technique, not a claimed new algorithmic
paradigm. A state-space breadth-first search also provides an exact finite method. For a fixed

4.4   A discovered spatial topology

We also leave the fixed row architecture. Consider two 3 × 2 cell grids, one before service and
one after it, with a source entering the middle-left approach cell and an exit reachable from the
middle-right departure cell. Every grid interface is optional and bidirectional with shared unit
capacity. Each cell stores two people. Counter service times at top, middle, and bottom sites are
(2, 4, 3) and N = 8.
The optimum is 17 ticks, with eight selected corridors. At that deadline, the minimum illustrative
construction cost is 2,200 and the additional area is 28 m2 . The selected network branches before
service and merges afterward; neither branch structure was prescribed. Horizon 16 is solver-certified
infeasible. The selection is global over all 216 portal subsets, optional cells, and counter-use choices,
although branch-and-bound does not explicitly visit every subset.

6     Sensitivity and operating policies

6.1   Receiving-space reuse changes the theorem

Under turnover receiving, retain Ov (t) ≤ nv (t) but replace the space bound by

                                        Iv (t) ≤ Kv − nv (t) + Ov (t).

movement. It only permits an incoming person to reuse a place vacated during that tick.

for every KM ≥ 1 and Q0 ≥ 1.

Proof. The exit can now accept and discharge in consecutive ticks with one storage place; the
one-place departure cells also support successive releases one tick apart. Consequently δ = 1 and
b = p. The counting lower bounds and direct staggered construction in Theorem 1 apply with these
substitutions. Approach cells still have storage two and source admissions remain at most one per
tick.

For N = 12, m = 3, p = 3, KM = 1, the optimum changes from 30 to 19 solely by this convention.
This is a decisive model assumption, not a universal claim that a real one-place merge halves
capacity. Hardware FIFO documentation explicitly distinguishes simultaneous operations, flag
state, and latency [10]; a hardware application must select the corresponding receiving semantics.

6.3   Static versus dynamically opened connections

For the original N = 12, p = 3, KM = 2, Q0 = 3 instance, separate direct lanes attain 19 ticks
with six portals, a selected two-feeder design attains 19 with five, and a central pooled feeder with
four portals needs 20. For (p1 , p2 , p3 ) = (1, 3, 5) the same three fixed designs take 18, 19, and 20,
respectively. However, another five-portal design attains 18. The identity of a sparse design matters
when service times differ.
If constructed portals have no adverse physical effect when unused, a static graph containing the
union of all connections used by a dynamic schedule can execute that same schedule. Thus dynamic
opening cannot improve the unconstrained optimum or the optimum under a budget on the union
of constructed connections. This monotonicity is a property of centrally controlled routing, not a
statement about selfish routing equilibria.
Different answers arise under a limit on simultaneously active approach portals. In the uniform
instance, limits of one, two, and three active portals per tick give optima of 30, 19, and 19. The
respective minimum installed counts at those deadlines are four, six, and five. The dynamic model
assumes costless instantaneous opening and closing and still charges all installed portals. It does
not model moving walls, switching setup time, or physical reconnection work.


6.4   Random durations and decentralized policies

Assign each of the 12 jobs an independent service duration equal to 1 or 5 with equal probability,
independent of which counter serves it. This is a stochastic uniform-worker experiment, separate
from the heterogeneous deterministic experiments. Each job retains its duration when policies are
compared. Controllers observe populations and service completion, but not future durations or
remaining durations. All 4,096 possible job-duration assignments are evaluated for each of seven
policies, with independent event replay for every trajectory.

8    What this can contribute to networks

The cleanest transfer is to a controlled, lossless finite batch moving through finite buffers and
alternative workers. A person becomes a packet or task, a counter a packet processor or accelerator,
a waiting cell a FIFO or staging buffer, a portal an interconnect, and the merge a shared output
channel. Heterogeneous pj describe different worker speeds. Area and construction budgets become
buffer memory, hardware area, link cost, or a weighted combination supplied by the designer.
Three concrete questions are now executable in the finite model: how few interconnects preserve
burst-drain time; whether an additional buffer slot is more valuable than another worker; and
which worker placement and admission schedule minimize clearance under an area or cost budget.
The robust 27-tick schedule also illustrates a deterministic finite-burst completion guarantee when
processing times are bounded.
These are proposed applications rather than validated hardware results. General Internet traffic,
packet drops and retransmission, several service stages, multiple destinations, fairness, long-run
stability, and decentralized strategic routing are not covered by this single-service, single-exit model.
FIFO timing must be matched to the actual implementation, including credit-return or flag latency.
The mathematical framework is useful precisely because those receiving and capacity choices can
be stated, varied, and checked rather than hidden inside a generic queue label.
